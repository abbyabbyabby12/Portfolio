Web Development

Semester 2 Class 2026

What the class was about

ITWD5.130 Website Development taught me how to take a website from an idea through planning, design, and development, using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The assessment followed the website development life cycle in two parts. Part 1 was a Website Specification Document, covering the website's definition and requirements (purpose, audience, devices, functionality, technologies, and comparable websites) and its design (sitemap, wireframes, and design principles). Part 2 was building the static website I had designed, then writing a review and evaluation of the finished site. The website had to use semantic HTML, a single CSS file, responsive layouts with media queries, accessibility features such as alt text, and at least two interactive elements. I chose to design a drinks recipe website called The Recipe Lounge.

What I learnt

In this course I learned that good websites start with planning, because defining a clear purpose, target audience, and measurable goals shapes every later design decision. I learned to analyse comparable websites (Difford's Guide, Mocktail.net, and Smoothie Recipes) for their design, content, interactions, and accessibility, and to use that research to define my own unique selling proposition. I learned how a sitemap sets out a website's information hierarchy and how wireframes show layout and key elements before any code is written. I also learned to design for different devices by creating separate smartphone and desktop wireframes for each page, and to explain my choices using design principles such as visual hierarchy, proximity, alignment, consistency, repetition, white space, and typographic hierarchy. I learned to weigh the advantages and disadvantages of my own designs, for example noticing that a side navigation takes up valuable phone screen space, or that a wide layout could leave too much empty space on a large monitor. I learned to choose a toolchain for each phase (Excalidraw and Figma for design, VS Code and Chrome Developer Tools for coding and debugging, and GitHub Pages for hosting), and to reference sources and disclose AI use in APA style.

What I achieved

In Part 1, Section 1, I wrote the specification for The Recipe Lounge, a drinks recipe website covering categories such as cocktails, mocktails, smoothies, milkshakes, coffee, hot drinks, and iced teas. I defined the website's purpose and scope and set measurable goals, including multiple linked pages, a simple browsing and search interface, a working favourites feature, print and share buttons, and a review space on each recipe page. I described four audience groups (teenagers and young adults, adults and home entertainers, families, and drink enthusiasts) and explained how my content and design would meet their needs on smartphones, tablets, laptops, and desktops. I documented five use cases: exploring recipes, searching and filtering, contacting the website owner, reviewing recipes, and discovering featured drinks. I also listed my web technologies. I compared three existing websites, covering design, content, interactions, and accessibility, and wrote a unique selling proposition based on offering a wide variety of drink categories in one beginner-friendly, responsive website. In Part 1, Section 2, I designed the website's structure and layout. I created a sitemap with the homepage as the entry point, and designed seven pages: Home, Browse Recipes, Favourites, Recipe Details, My Account, About Us, and Contact Us. Each page has both a smartphone wireframe and a desktop/tablet wireframe, which gives 14 wireframes in total. Every wireframe is annotated with design principles, a consistent purple, light blue, pale yellow, and white colour palette, and a comment on its advantages and disadvantages. I included APA references throughout. In Part 2, I built the static website from this design and wrote the review and evaluation document. Reflections

This course showed me how much of website development happens before writing code, because my specification and wireframes gave me a clear plan for building the pages and keeping them consistent. Researching comparable websites taught me to look critically at what works well and what doesn't, such as crowded layouts and poor colour contrast, and to avoid repeating those mistakes in my own design. Writing the design annotations also made me think carefully about why a layout works, rather than just choosing something that looks good. I found it challenging to design a recipe website that is rich in content, with filters, favourites, reviews, and accounts, while keeping the scope realistic for a static website, so next time I'd prioritise the essential features earlier and treat the rest as future improvements. I also want to build my confidence in CSS layout, accessibility, and JavaScript interactions. Overall, this course gave me a practical process for planning and building websites, and it will help me in future web design and development work.
