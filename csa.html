Computer System Architecture

Semester 2 Class 2026

What the class was about

ITCS5.100 Computer Systems Architecture taught me how to plan, construct, optimise, and maintain a modern PC-based computer system, using safe and effective industry practices. I covered computer hardware, health and safety, documentation and diagram interpretation, problem solving, diagnostics and benchmarking, operating systems, security, networking, and firewalls. The practical assessment (70% of the grade) was split into two parts. Part 1 was assembling and troubleshooting a PC, and Part 2 was scripting and operating system maintenance. For each part I completed five labs in a small team, submitting my own write-up each week. A final test (30%) covered all the topics.

What I learnt

In this course I learned how the parts of a computer fit together and how to choose hardware for a purpose and budget, planning both an entry-level system and a custom power-user PC and justifying my component choices. I learned the practical side of being a technician, including safe component replacement, cleaning and maintenance routines, and ergonomic workstation design, and I learned to write system administration documentation in Markdown and troubleshoot faults systematically by recording symptoms, causes, effects, and solutions. I also learned how diagnostic and benchmarking tools help find and fix problems, and how operating systems manage processes, scheduling, and security. In the second half, I learned how security tools such as Kali Linux and nmap are used to scan networks and find open ports, and how to automate network checks and manage firewall rules and logging with PowerShell, batch scripts, and command-line tools like netsh, auditpol, and wevtutil. I also saw how protocols such as SNMP are used to monitor devices, and why firewalls and end-point protection sit outside penetration-testing toolsets.

What I achieved

In Part 1, I started with a hardware investigation of an old EIT PC. I identified its Intel AF82801JDO (ICH10 DO) chipset, compared the block diagrams of the Intel P45 and G33 chipsets, and compared the chipset's specification with how it is implemented on the board. I also researched supported processor families with their S-SPEC codes, the impact of ARM processors, and memory standards, speeds, bandwidth, latency, and timing. For computer assembly, I recommended three entry-level laptops close to the NZ$1500 target (the ASUS Vivobook 16, Lenovo ThinkBook 16 Gen 9, and Dell 15 DC15250). I also planned a custom VR and video-editing PC, centred on a Ryzen 9 9950X, an RTX 5070 Ti, 64GB of RAM, and an 850W power supply, with a full parts list costing NZ$7,162.39 including shipping. I explained my component choices and how I would check compatibility. In the health and safety lab, I listed user-replaceable parts, pre-replacement steps, and a technician's toolkit. I also covered cleaning routines for computers, monitors, and shared peripherals, and the risks of using an air compressor, and I drew an ergonomic workstation layout in draw.io. For documentation, I wrote a step-by-step Markdown guide, with screenshots, to install Windows 11 Pro in an Oracle VirtualBox virtual machine. In the problem-solving lab, I documented five build faults (RAM, CPU, GPU, PSU, and SSD installation issues) using a symptoms, causes, effects, and solutions format, and identified five faults that could cause a working PC to fail. In the diagnostics lab, I explained the role of Hiren's BootCD and demonstrated Task Manager, the Windows Memory Diagnostic tool, PowerShell, and Command Prompt with screenshots. In Part 2, I explained why firewalls and end-point protection aren't part of Kali Linux, and I identified five Kali tools that could be scripted for end-point protection (ClamAV, Rootkit Hunter, chkrootkit, Lynis, and YARA). I installed nmap and wrote a PowerShell script that scans my local network for devices and open ports and saves the results to a file. I then identified a selection of Windows, NirSoft, and Sysinternals network tools and wrote a NetworkAudit.ps1 script that saves my network settings to a text file. I set it up in Task Scheduler to run at logon and after a group policy update, and I tested it with gpupdate /force. For protocols, I used Wireshark to capture 60 seconds of traffic on my Wi-Fi connection, identified 18 protocols (including QUIC, TCP, UDP, DNS, TLS, ARP, and mDNS), and described what each one does. In the final lab, I wrote a script that uses netsh to forward port 80 to 8080 on my local address, enables firewall logging, and uses wevtutil to export firewall-related security events to a text file. Every lab was documented with screenshots, my scripts, and APA references.

Reflections

I came into this course with almost no hands-on hardware knowledge, and I was upfront about that in my first labs, so I'm proud of how far I've come, from identifying a chipset on an old PC to writing scripts that scan a network, schedule an audit, and configure firewall settings. Writing the Windows 11 installation guide in Markdown taught me how much clear, step-by-step documentation matters, because every missed screenshot or step made the guide harder to follow. I found scripting the most challenging part, especially making PowerShell scripts run correctly and trigger on a schedule, and I relied on AI tools and online guides to get started, so I'd like to practise writing and debugging scripts on my own until I fully understand each line. In my firewall lab, my script ran but found no firewall events to export, which taught me that a result of "nothing found" still needs to be checked and explained rather than assumed to be a success. I also learned to be careful when researching hardware, because chipset and memory details varied between sources, so next time I'd verify specifications against manufacturer documentation. Overall, this course gave me practical confidence with the tools technicians use and showed me how hardware, scripting, security, and networking connect, which will help me in a future support or system administration role.
