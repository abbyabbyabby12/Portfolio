Data Concepts

Semester 1 Class 2026

What the class was about

ITDT5.228 Introduction to Data Concepts covered how data is represented, structured, coded, stored, and protected. The weekly topics were number systems (binary, octal, hexadecimal, and base conversion), Boolean logic and truth tables, data structures (arrays, linked lists, stacks, queues, trees, and graphs), coding systems and barcodes, and character encoding (ASCII) and hash tables. The practical assignment followed a database for a fictional event company, Armageddon Expos, through four parts. It began with entity relationship design and business rules, then moved to improving the ERD, then to building the database with SQL (DDL and DML), and finished with a research report on legal and ethical issues under the NZ Privacy Act 2020.

What I learnt

I learned how computers represent and process data at a basic level. I could count and convert between number systems, use Boolean operators and truth tables to make binary decisions and query data, and explain how different data structures organise data for different jobs. I also learned how coding systems like bank account numbers, room codes, and barcodes pack meaning into a compact format, and how ASCII and hash functions turn characters into numbers that index data quickly. In the database work, I learned to design before building. I identified entities, primary keys, attributes, and foreign keys, and described business rules and cardinality between entities. I learned why many-to-many relationships cause duplicated and incorrect results in relational databases, and how junction (bridge) tables resolve them into one-to-many relationships. I also learned to choose appropriate data types and keep naming consistent. In SQL, I learned to create a database and tables, insert and update records, and write queries that filter, sort, group, count, and calculate values. In the privacy research, I learned how the 13 Privacy Act 2020 principles apply to a real organisation's data and how to support points with APA references.

What I achieved

Across the four practical parts, I designed, built, and researched a database for Armageddon Expos. In Part 1, I defined the entities (Attendees, Tickets, Venues, Exhibitors, Stalls, Events, Celebrity Guests, and Categories) with their primary keys and attributes, and wrote business rules describing how they relate. In Part 2, I improved the ERD by resolving the many-to-many relationships with bridge tables, adding a Sponsor entity with an EventSponsorship table for sponsorship amounts, signage, branding, and ticket allocations, and adding Staff and Rosters to cover staff working at events. In Part 3, I turned the ERD into a working MySQL database called dbARMAGEDDON, creating all 15 tables with primary keys, foreign keys, and constraints such as NOT NULL and CHECK rules. I imported data from CSV files, inserted and updated staff and roster records, and wrote queries that sorted and filtered staff, detected incorrectly entered names using a pattern match, calculated afternoon pay rates, and used joins and GROUP BY to list staff by station and event and count staff and events. Each task was documented with the SQL, screenshots of the results, and a full script. In Part 4, I wrote a research report relating the 13 Privacy Act 2020 principles to Armageddon's data, summarised three NZ privacy breaches (Latitude, ACC, and Manage My Health), and recommended two compliance actions: strong database security with role-based access controls, and privacy training with clear privacy policies. I supported this with APA references.

Reflections

This course showed me that data work covers more than storing and querying information. It also means designing structures carefully, understanding how data is represented, and keeping it safe. Working through the Armageddon database from ERD to SQL showed me why careful design matters, and that small details count when building tables, such as creating them in dependency order and making sure foreign keys point to the correct table names. I found writing queries that combine several tables, filters, and calculations the most challenging part, so I'd like to keep practising joins, grouping, and checking results against expected output. The privacy research changed how I think about design, because security, retention, and access controls need to be planned from the start rather than added later. Together with my SQL and design skills, that perspective will help me build and manage systems that are both useful and responsible with people's information.
